Signals → what to animate

Every signal is a real FFT-based measurement, not a guess. Here's the field guide to turning each one into motion.

Grid vs hits

beats[] is the expected grid — uniform, phase-locked to the kick, safe for cuts and quantization. onsets[] are the actual transients — ragged and expressive, perfect for impact FX. Use the grid for structure, hits for drama.

Low bpmConfidence (< 0.5)

The pulse is unclear (ambient, live drums, rubato). The grid may drift — prefer onsets[] for such tracks, or constrain the tempo search with --min-bpm/--max-bpm. Pulseless material reports bpm: 0 — never an invented tempo.

Reading the bands

Each of the six frequency bands is normalized against its own history, so every band shows its full 0–1 dynamics range — bass tracks don't drown out the treble, and quiet songs still light up the bands when they hit. bands.matrix is one row per analysis frame (~43 fps internally); bandsByFrame (present when you pass --fps) resamples it to exactly one row per video frame, aligned with useCurrentFrame() in Remotion or hf-seek in HyperFrames.

The classic two-channel rig

Most audio-reactive looks run on two signals: bass → scale/glow (slow, weighty) and treble → sparkle/detail (fast, sharp). Add centroid as a third channel for color and everything breathes with the track.

Composing a look

Three recipes that cover most music videos and beat visuals:

  • Pulse — beats[] drives cut points; beats[].strength scales the pop; bass band drives a soft radial glow behind the subject.
  • Strobe — onsets[] trigger 1–2 frame white/glitch flashes; highMid intensity gates the flashes so weak hits stay quiet.
  • Evolve — sections[] change the palette/scene; centroid drifts the grade warm/cool; loudness[] rides the overall scale and opacity.

Deciding where the energy should go? Run spectrumalyzer report first — the heatmap shows the song's anatomy before you animate anything.
